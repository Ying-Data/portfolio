All suppliers and amounts are fabricated. The client's figures stay confidential; the logic is the real thing.

Step 1, the problem

Two systems, one set of money

Paper invoices were keyed into ExpertM. Digital invoices flowed into Odoo. Every month they disagreed: same suppliers spelled three ways, duplicates, a payment with no reference. Finding out why took two days of side-by-side checking.

Step 2, reconcile

Exact matches first, no judgment calls

Reference, supplier and amount agree on both sides: matched and set aside. This clears most of the volume in seconds and, more importantly, gets it out of the way of the rows that need attention.

Step 3, reconcile the hard rows

Fuzzy matches, each with its reason on the label

The remainder pairs on rules a reviewer can audit: same amount, supplier name variant. Same amount, date shifted. Nothing matches silently; every pairing carries the reason it was allowed.

Step 4, reduce

What is left is the real work

A cash payment with no reference and a duplicate entry stay in the exception queue, flagged for a human decision. A reconciliation that claims one hundred percent is hiding something. This one shows its residue.

Step 5, read

One page a partner reads in a minute

The output is not a spreadsheet, it is a decision: what matched, what matched with a reason, what needs you, and a difference of 0,00 once the exceptions are resolved. The cycle that took two days now takes two hours, and the checking became repeatable instead of heroic.

03The presenter

I build the analysis and I present it myself. Nobody translates for me.

The part of BI nobody screens for is the moment the analyst has to stand up and make a room act. I trained that part deliberately, on real stages, in a country that was new to me. The photos below are from Clusity's Summer Gathering, the women-in-tech community's five-year celebration, where I was one of nine keynote speakers. My talk, "Both.", makes the case that the analyst who can read the room and read the data is the rare, useful one.

Scene one. I could barely hold my notes.

My first Toastmasters evening in Belgium: new country, second language, a room of strangers. I kept going back. My coach tells the rest of that story below, in his own words.

Scene two. The club put me in charge of its voice.

A year later I was elected VP of Public Relations for Toastmasters Belgium: running the club's messaging, and spending evenings helping other nervous people make one clear point to a room. That is most of what BI actually is once the SQL runs.

Scene three. The stage changed how I work.

Stand-up taught me the discipline a dashboard never will: cut what does not land and say the one thing that does, in words a tired person gets the first time. Bomb on stage and you know instantly. Ship a report nobody reads and you find out in three months. I prefer the honest feedback loop.
